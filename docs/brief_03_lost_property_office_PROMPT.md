# Claude Code Prompt — "The Lost Property Office" (20-min animated film)

You are the **director, writer, and technical lead** for an original ~20-minute animated short film for families. Children (6–12) should love the endless shelves of lost things, the fussy paper clerks and the kite companion; adults should recognise it as a film about grief and letting go, without the film ever saying so. Part A is the creative brief. Part B is the production pipeline you must build and run.

---

# PART A — CREATIVE BRIEF

## A1. Logline
A month after her grandfather dies, a girl who lost his old station-master's pocket watch boards a midnight train made of luggage tags to the Lost Property Office in the clouds — where everything ever lost is filed away, and you may only take back what you're truly ready to lose again.

## A2. The two layers
- **Kid layer:** a magical train, an infinite office of lost things (umbrellas, single socks, milk teeth, lost tempers in jars), a fussy head clerk, forms in triplicate, a lost kite who becomes a friend, a race against fading.
- **Adult layer:** grief. Holding on too tightly. The things we never said. The idea that what we love isn't kept by grasping it. The words "death", "died" and "grief" must never be spoken in the film. Handle it through the watch, the empty chair and Dadaji's line.

## A3. Influences (techniques only — story, characters and world are entirely original)
Classic British children's-fantasy techniques: a child travelling alone into a magical world next door to ordinary life; strict, slightly absurd rules run by eccentric adults; a ticking deadline; a safe return home with something gained.

## A4. Setting
**Real world:** a small Indian railway junction town — an old station with a red-and-cream building, a big clock that's always five minutes slow, a tea stall, a footbridge, and a disused siding beyond the last platform. Anaya's family home is near the station; Dadaji was the station master for 40 years. His chair on the veranda is now empty.
**The other world:** the Lost Property Office — a colossal building floating in moonlit clouds, a mix of Indian railway bureaucracy and endless library. Brass lamps, green-shaded desks, rubber stamps, ledgers taller than houses, pneumatic tubes, rolling ladders, shelves that rise until they dissolve into the night sky as stars. **Every clock in the Office is stopped — except, somewhere, one watch.**

## A5. Characters (keep cast small)
| Name | Who | Arc / function |
|---|---|---|
| **Anaya** (10) | Quiet, stubborn, fiercely loyal; short hair, Dadaji's too-big railway cardigan over her clothes | From grasping tightly to letting go — and from silence to saying it |
| **Dadaji** (flashbacks + voice) | Retired station master; white moustache, spectacles on a string, always winding his pocket watch | His line is the film's spine: *"Time is for spending, beta, not for keeping."* |
| **Bade Babu** | Head clerk of the Office — tall, thin, made of folded paper, owl-like spectacles, a rubber stamp for a hand; fussy, rule-bound, secretly kind | The rules, and the comedy of bureaucracy; the gatekeeper |
| **Chhota Babu** | Junior clerk — small, round, ink-stained paper, eager, always dropping files | Comic relief; helps Anaya break a few small rules |
| **Patang** | A lost paper kite, faded pink with a torn tail and a broken string; moves and gestures, no words | Her companion and mirror: lost too, longs to fly again |

## A6. World rules (introduce through action)
1. Everything ever lost ends up in the Office, filed and catalogued.
2. You can only get there if you've lost something that truly matters: the **Luggage-Tag Express** arrives at the disused siding at midnight for those people.
3. You may take back only what you're **truly ready to lose again**. Bade Babu's stamp simply will not come down otherwise.
4. Things never claimed slowly rise up the shelves and become stars.
5. Visitors who stay too long start to get lost themselves: a luggage tag appears on your wrist and your colours fade.

## A7. Beat sheet (target timings — runtime 20:00)
1. **00:00–02:00 — Before.** Warm flashback: Dadaji and little Anaya on the platform, he lets her wind the watch: "Time is for spending, beta, not for keeping." Cut to now: the house is quiet, his veranda chair empty, relatives speaking softly. Anaya tears her room apart looking for the watch she lost at the station.
2. **02:00–04:00 — The train.** Midnight. She sneaks to the station and, at the disused siding, a train built of thousands of fluttering luggage tags pulls in. Its doors open just for her.
3. **04:00–07:00 — The Office.** Arrival in the clouds: the big set-piece of infinite shelves. Bade Babu and his forms ("Form 7-B, Item Lost, Sentimental, in triplicate"). Comic tour: the Department of Single Socks, a shelf of lost milk teeth, jars of lost tempers rattling, a lost voice whispering from a drawer. Chhota Babu takes a shine to her.
4. **07:00–10:00 — The search.** The ledger says the watch is filed in a department far above. Journey up rolling ladders and through pneumatic tubes. She meets Patang, a lost kite tangled in a shelf, and frees it; it follows her. They pass the **Department of Things Never Said**: shelves of sealed glass jars, each holding a faint glowing whisper.
5. **10:00–13:00 — Found.** At the top, among the stopped clocks, the only ticking thing: Dadaji's watch. Anaya clutches it. Bade Babu arrives: "Are you ready to lose it again?" She says "Yes" — and the stamp will not come down. It knows.
6. **13:00–16:00 — Holding on.** She runs with the watch. The Office reacts: shelves begin dissolving into stars around her, a luggage tag appears on her wrist, her colours start to fade, and she's being filed as lost. Patang drags her out of a collapsing aisle — and tears itself further doing it. They tumble into the Department of Things Never Said. One jar glows with her own handwriting on the label: *"Anaya — to Dadaji."* Inside: the goodbye she never said.
7. **16:00–18:00 — Climax.** She opens the jar and says it out loud (short, simple, devastating; write it with restraint). The Office goes still. Her colour returns. She understands Dadaji's line. She looks at Patang, broken and longing for the sky, and makes a choice: she asks Bade Babu to let Patang be **found** instead, and returns the kite to the wind. Then she holds out the watch, and this time she truly means it. The stamp comes down: *RETURNED TO OWNER.*
8. **18:00–19:30 — Home.** Dawn at the station; the luggage-tag train dissolves into fluttering paper. The watch is ticking in her hand. Far away, a little boy in another town looks up as a faded pink kite drifts down into his hands. In the sky, a new star fades into the morning.
9. **19:30–20:00 — Coda.** On the veranda, Anaya teaches her small cousin to wind the watch — then puts it in his hands to keep. "Time is for spending." She sits in Dadaji's chair. Final insert: in the Office, Bade Babu writes in the ledger — *"One (1) heavy heart. Returned to owner. Lighter."* Credits.

## A8. Visual style
Soft watercolour-and-ink look with visible paper texture and gentle ink outlines; simple, rounded character designs with clear silhouettes.
**Two palettes:** the real world is dusty and muted — faded sepia, railway red and cream, pale morning blue — and the flashbacks are warm golden. The Office is moonlit paper-white, ink blue and polished brass, with stars glowing at the top of the shelves. Anaya's fading is shown by desaturation toward grey; her recovery returns full colour. Patang is the one bright pink note in the Office.
Camera: vast vertical scale in the Office (tiny Anaya, towering shelves); intimate, still framing at home; the watch shot as a hero object in close-up with shallow focus.

## A9. Sound & music
Instruments: solo piano, sarangi, soft strings, a music box.
Leitmotifs: **(1) Dadaji's theme** — a simple, warm melody first heard on the platform, played on the watch's chime as a music box; it returns in full at the climax. **(2) The Office** — playful, clockwork-pizzicato bureaucracy music for Bade Babu. **(3) Patang's theme** — airy, rising flute line.
**Ticking is a musical element:** the Office is silent of clocks except the watch's tick, which grows louder as she nears it and which the score locks to in the climax.
Soundscape: station announcements in the distance, the tea-seller's call, the fluttering of thousands of luggage tags, stamps thudding, pneumatic tubes whooshing.

## A10. Tone guardrails
This is the most emotional of the films — earn it with comedy first (the Office must be genuinely funny for the first half). No on-screen death, no funeral imagery, no illness. The grief lives in the empty chair, the watch and the unsaid goodbye. The climax line must be short and plain; resist melodrama. The ending is warm, not sad.

# PART B — PRODUCTION PIPELINE

You are building a **resumable, manifest-driven production pipeline** that turns the creative brief above into a finished ~20-minute animated film. Treat this like a real studio production: story is locked before pictures, pictures are locked before expensive renders, and nothing costly runs without my approval.

## B0. Ground rules (read first)

1. **Gates are mandatory.** Stop and ask me for approval at every `🛑 GATE`. Show me the artefact (file path + a short summary), then wait. Never skip a gate to "save time".
2. **Budget is a hard cap.** Every paid API call is logged to `logs/costs.csv` (timestamp, phase, shot_id, provider, model, units, est_cost_usd). Before each phase, print projected spend vs. remaining budget. If a call would exceed `config.yaml → budget_usd`, halt and ask.
3. **Idempotent and resumable.** Every asset is keyed by shot ID + content hash of its prompt/inputs. Re-running a command never regenerates an approved asset. A crash mid-run resumes from the manifest.
4. **Provider-agnostic.** The AI video/image/voice/music landscape changes monthly. Do **not** hard-code one vendor. Write thin adapter classes (`ImageProvider`, `VideoProvider`, `VoiceProvider`, `MusicProvider`) so I can swap models via config.
5. **Store every prompt with its output.** Each generated file gets a sidecar `.json` with the exact prompt, model, seed, reference images, and cost. This is how we debug drift and reproduce good takes.
6. **Original work only.** No real people's likenesses, no named living artists or studios in style prompts, no existing copyrighted characters, no copyrighted music or melodies. Describe the style in plain visual terms (the style bible does this).
7. **Child audience.** No graphic peril, no gore, nothing frightening beyond mild suspense. When in doubt, gentler.

## B1. Phase 0 — Setup & research (no spend)

1. Check the environment: Python 3.11+, `ffmpeg` (with libx264, libass), `git`. Install what's missing.
2. Scaffold the project:

```
film/
  config.yaml            # budget, resolution, fps, language, providers, takes_per_shot
  .env                   # API keys — gitignored, never printed
  bible/
    story.md             # Part A of this prompt, verbatim
    style.md             # visual style bible (Phase 2)
    characters/<name>/   # canonical text block + reference sheets
    locations/<name>/    # canonical text block + reference plates
  script/script.md
  shots/shots.json       # the single source of truth for every shot
  assets/{keyframes,clips,voice,music,sfx}/
  edit/{animatic,rough,final}/
  logs/{costs.csv,manifest.json,qc.csv}
  src/film/              # pipeline code (CLI below)
```

3. CLI (`python -m film <cmd>`): `status`, `estimate`, `script`, `bible`, `shots`, `keyframes [--scene N]`, `animatic`, `render [--scene N] [--shot ID]`, `qc`, `voice`, `music`, `sfx`, `assemble`, `export`. Every paid command supports `--dry-run`.
4. **Research current tools.** Use web search to find the best currently available APIs for: (a) image generation with strong character consistency via reference images, (b) image-to-video with reference/first-frame conditioning, (c) expressive multi-voice TTS (English + Hindi), (d) instrumental music generation with commercial-use rights, (e) sound effects. For each, record model name, price per unit, max clip length, licence terms for commercial use, and any content restrictions. Put this in `bible/tooling.md`.
5. Produce `python -m film estimate`: projected cost for the whole film assuming ~180–220 shots, average 6 s, `takes_per_shot` retakes, plus voice/music/sfx. Give low / expected / high.

🛑 **GATE 0** — Show me `tooling.md`, the recommended stack, and the cost estimate. Ask for: budget cap, API keys, language (default: English with light natural Hindi), and resolution (default 1920×1080, 24 fps).

## B2. Phase 1 — Screenplay (no spend)

1. Write `script/script.md` in standard screenplay format from the beat sheet in Part A. Target **20 pages ≈ 20:00 runtime** (±30 s, excluding credits).
2. Dialogue rules: sparse, short lines, mostly visual storytelling. Kids should be able to follow it with the sound off. Adults should catch the second layer without it ever being stated.
3. Add a **lip-sync budget**: tag each line `ON` (face visible, mouth must sync) or `OFF` (voiceover, back of head, wide shot, character off-screen). Aim for **≤25% ON lines** — this hugely reduces cost and failure rate.
4. Write a one-page `script/logline_and_themes.md`: logline, kid layer, adult layer, the one image that would be the poster.

🛑 **GATE 1** — Script approval. I may request revisions; iterate until approved. The story is locked after this.

## B3. Phase 2 — Style & character bibles (small spend)

1. `bible/style.md`: the visual style from Part A expanded into: rendering style, line quality, texture, lighting rules per time of day, colour palette (hex codes per scene/mood), camera grammar (lenses, movement rules, typical shot sizes), what to avoid.
2. For every character: a **canonical text block** (≤80 words, identical every time it's used — never paraphrase it), then generate a reference sheet: front, 3/4, side, back, 4 expressions. For every recurring location: an establishing plate + 2 alternate angles.
3. Generate 3–4 candidates per character/location, then pick the best using your own visual judgement and explain why. Lock the winners into `bible/characters/<name>/ref_*.png`.

**Consistency strategy (apply everywhere downstream):**
- Every image/video prompt = `[style block] + [canonical character blocks for who's in frame] + [location block] + [shot-specific action]`. Assemble programmatically from the bible; never hand-write character descriptions in shot prompts.
- Always pass the locked reference images to the model.
- Prefer **image-to-video from an approved keyframe** over text-to-video.
- Keep shots short (3–8 s). Avoid: crowds, close-ups of hands, fast complex action, text in frame, more than 3 characters in frame.

🛑 **GATE 2** — Show me a contact sheet (`bible/contact_sheet.png`) of all characters and locations. Lock after approval.

## B4. Phase 3 — Shot list (no spend)

Break the script into `shots/shots.json`. One object per shot:

```json
{
  "id": "S03_SH014",
  "scene": 3,
  "duration_s": 5.0,
  "shot_size": "MS",
  "camera": "slow push-in",
  "characters": ["meher", "chutney"],
  "location": "gali_night",
  "action": "Meher kneels and offers the dog her water bottle; the dog drinks.",
  "dialogue": [{"char": "meher", "line": "...", "sync": "OFF"}],
  "sfx": ["distant traffic", "water lapping"],
  "music_cue": "M03_wonder_theme",
  "transition_in": "cut",
  "notes": "Key emotional beat; hold an extra beat at end."
}
```

Validate: total duration within ±30 s of target; every script line is covered; no shot breaks the consistency rules above; scene-by-scene runtime table printed.

## B5. Phase 4 — Keyframes & animatic (moderate spend — the cheapest place to fix the film)

1. Generate one keyframe still per shot (2 candidates; auto-pick with QC below).
2. Generate scratch voices with cheap TTS.
3. Cut `edit/animatic/animatic.mp4`: keyframes held for shot duration with gentle Ken Burns moves, scratch voices, temp music beds, burned-in shot IDs and timecode.

🛑 **GATE 3** — Animatic review. This is where pacing, clarity and emotional beats get fixed. Re-cut and regenerate keyframes as I request. **Picture is locked after this.**

## B6. Phase 5 — Pilot scene (controlled spend)

Render ONE complete scene (pick the most visually demanding, ~1.5–2.5 min) to full final quality: video clips, final voices, music, sfx, mixed. Report: actual cost per finished second, retake rate, consistency issues seen, and a revised full-film estimate.

🛑 **GATE 4** — Pilot approval + revised budget confirmation. If quality isn't there, propose concrete fixes (different model, simpler shots, style adjustment) before scaling.

## B7. Phase 6 — Full production

1. **Video:** image-to-video per shot from its approved keyframe, `takes_per_shot` takes (default 2). Run concurrently within provider rate limits, with exponential-backoff retries.
2. **Automated QC (`python -m film qc`)** — for each take, extract frames at 0%, 50%, 100% and inspect them yourself against the character reference sheets and style bible. Score 1–5 on: character consistency, style consistency, action matches shot description, artefacts (morphing, extra limbs, warping faces, flicker). Log to `logs/qc.csv`. Auto-select the best take ≥4; if none, retake up to 2 more times with an adjusted prompt; if still failing, add to `logs/human_review.md` and move on — never block the whole run on one shot.
3. **Voice:** one locked voice ID per character (chosen at GATE 2 from 3 auditions each — add voice auditions to that gate). Generate final lines with performance direction from the script. Lip-sync only `ON` lines.
4. **Music:** compose (via the music model) a small set of leitmotifs defined in Part A, then scene cues built from those motifs so the score feels like one piece of music, not 20 random tracks. Instrumental only. Confirm commercial-use licence.
5. **SFX:** ambience bed per location + spot effects per shot.

Print progress and spend after every 10 shots.

## B8. Phase 7 — Edit, mix, finish

1. Assemble with ffmpeg from `shots.json` order: clips trimmed to duration, transitions as specified, colour-matched across shots (simple per-scene LUT or grade to the style bible palette).
2. Mix: dialogue clear above music; music ducks under dialogue; loudness normalised to −16 LUFS integrated (streaming) and a −23 LUFS broadcast version.
3. Opening title card + end credits (list every AI tool used, per their licence requirements).
4. Subtitles: `.srt` in English and Hindi.

🛑 **GATE 5** — Rough cut review. Fix list → final.

## B9. Deliverables

- `edit/final/<title>_1080p.mp4` (H.264, high quality) + `_4k.mp4` if the source supports it
- `edit/final/<title>_en.srt`, `_hi.srt`
- `edit/final/trailer_60s.mp4` — cut from finished footage
- `edit/final/poster.png` (2:3) and `thumbnail.png` (16:9) — from the poster image in the logline doc
- `edit/final/stills/` — 10 best frames
- `logs/costs.csv` + a final spend summary
- `README.md` — how to re-render any shot, and what each command does

## B10. Definition of done

Runtime 19:30–20:30 excluding credits · no shot with QC score <3 in the final cut · every character recognisably the same across the whole film · dialogue intelligible · total spend ≤ approved budget · I've approved GATE 5.

**Start now with Phase 0. Your first message to me should be the GATE 0 report.**
